Problem and significance
The warning signs for all three groups happen at night, and today's tools need either a
sleep lab or something worn on the body.
RBD: a warning sign years ahead
People with RBD act out their dreams by shouting, punching or kicking, because the
normal muscle paralysis of REM sleep is lost.
In a 1,280-patient multicenter study, people with isolated RBD developed Parkinson's
disease, Lewy body dementia or multiple system atrophy at 6.3% per year; 73.5% had
converted after 12 years (Postuma et al., Brain 2019).
In the HypnoLaus general-population study, 21 of 1,997 adults (1.06%) met sleep-study
criteria for RBD (Haba-Rubio et al., SLEEP). Most people with RBD are never
diagnosed.
Diagnosis requires video-polysomnography with expert scoring, which does not scale
to screening or to finding patients for trials (Skjærbæk et al., 2026).
Questionnaires are cheap but flag many false positives from lookalikes such as sleep
apnea, nightmares, medications and NREM parasomnias (Cesari et al., J Sleep Res
2025).

Neuroprotective drug trials need 142–366 RBD patients per study arm, so finding these
people early is a bottleneck for research (Postuma et al., 2019).
Dementia: nights drive injuries and moves to nursing homes
An estimated 7.4 million Americans age 65 and older live with Alzheimer's dementia in
2026 (Alzheimer's Association).
Nighttime activity raises the risk of injury and unattended exits from the home, and in
one caregiver study it frequently led to nursing-home placement (Rowe et al.,
Alzheimer's & Dementia 2009).
Fragmented sleep signals risk: among 737 older adults, those in the top 10% for sleep
fragmentation had 1.5 times the risk of developing Alzheimer's compared with the
bottom 10% (Lim et al., Sleep 2013).
Autism: sleep problems are common and hard to measure
About 1 in 31 U.S. 8-year-olds has an autism diagnosis (CDC ADDM Network, 2022 data,
released April 2025).
Up to 81% of autistic children have sleep problems. When 214 autistic 2- to 6-year-olds
were asked to wear an activity tracker for 14 days, device intolerance cost 42% of the
cohort's data (Rapp et al., Sleep 2025).
The gap: no current tool screens for RBD, alerts dementia caregivers and tracks children's
sleep without something to wear or buy.
Prior work and novelty
Phone sonar for sleep is not new: SleepScore has used it since 2018. NightEcho's novelty is
what the sonar is used for (RBD screening, dementia alerts, children's sleep) and how the
model is trained before any patient data exists.

What NightEcho adds
1 . RBD screening from the phone's own speaker and microphone, with no wearable,
camera or bed sensor. Our searches found no published or commercial example; the
novelty check below must confirm this.
2. Lookalike gates. A REM gate, a breathing-pause check and a range gate each remove a
specific RBD mimic that defeats questionnaires.
3. A PSG-to-sonar simulator. Public sleep-lab recordings with confirmed diagnoses
become sonar-like training data, so the model learns RBD before any patient sleeps
beside a phone.
4. One sensing engine, three decision modules for RBD, dementia and autism.
5. Privacy by design. Raw audio never leaves the phone's memory and is never saved.

Two tracks run in parallel: the data track (Phases 1–7, on laptops) and the phone track
(Phase 8). They meet when the trained model is exported into the app.
Sonar sensing design
The phone sweeps an 18–22 kHz chirp every 10 ms. Echoes are sorted into 4.3 cm range
bins, and tiny phase shifts in the sleeper's bin reveal each breath.

The signal chain, in order
1 . Generate the chirp: a linear sweep from 18 to 22 kHz over 10 ms, with a 0.5 ms raised-
cosine taper at both ends, looped continuously at low volume.
2. Align frames: cross-correlate the recording with the chirp. The first strong peak is the
direct speaker-to-microphone path, which marks where each frame starts.
3. Dechirp: band-pass 18–22 kHz, form the analytic (complex) signal, and multiply by the
complex conjugate of the transmitted chirp.
4. Range FFT: one FFT per 10 ms frame gives a complex range profile with 4.3 cm bins.
5. Remove clutter: subtract each bin's 10-second moving average, which erases walls, the
bed frame and other still objects.
6. Lock the sleeper's bin: between 0.2 and 1.5 m, pick the bin with the most energy in the
0.1–0.7 Hz breathing band over 30 s. Switch only if another bin stays stronger for 60 s.
7. Breathing: unwrap the phase of the locked bin, band-pass it to 0.1–0.7 Hz and convert it
to displacement ( Δ d = λΔφ  / 4π).
8. Movement: sum the frame-to-frame change across bins from 0.2 to 1.5 m, and flag
bursts above median + 5 × MAD lasting at least 0.5 s.
9. Bed exit: large movement, then the sleeper's bin stays empty for at least 5 s, triggers
an alert.
1 0. Voice: low-pass to 8 kHz, resample to 16 kHz and run YAMNet. Keep only the labels and
timestamps, then discard the audio.
Known limits and safeguards
Obstructive apnea: chest effort can continue while airflow stops, so sonar sees falling
breathing amplitude and a large recovery breath, not a flat line. The breathing-pause
check therefore looks for amplitude falling at least 50% for 10 s or more, followed by a
recovery breath.
Children: young people often hear tones near 20 kHz. Pediatric mode sweeps 20–22
kHz, which coarsens the range bins to 8.6 cm, and an audibility check runs before the
first night.

Pets: dogs and cats hear well above 22 kHz. Keep the volume low and watch for

Phone differences: speakers and microphones fade near 20 kHz at different rates.
Measure each test phone first (step 8.2); if a phone can't reach 22 kHz, sweep 18–20

Power and heat: overnight playback and processing need the phone plugged in. Log

REM in people with RBD: movement during REM can make REM look like wake to a

Phases 4–6 turn features into decisions: a sleep stager that finds REM-like sleep, the RBD

Phase 4 · Sleep staging (Dec 7–20)
3.1 Implement the shared chain from the range–time map onward (Sonar sensing
design, steps 5–9) as one Python module. The phone app will copy it exactly.
3.2 Breathing features per 30-second epoch: rate (spectral peak), breath-to-breath
variability, depth (median peak-to-peak), regularity (autocorrelation peak) and change
in depth from the previous two epochs.
3.3 Movement features: burst count, total duration, peak energy (vigor), time since the
last burst, and whether bursts repeat every 5–90 s (the periodic leg movement
pattern).
3.4 Breathing-disturbance features: drops in amplitude of at least 50% lasting 10 s or
more, and the recovery breaths that follow.
3.5 Vocal features (real nights only): YAMNet counts per epoch for speech, shouting,
laughter, crying and snoring.
3.6 Context features: minutes since sleep onset, clock hour, and the two epochs before
and after.
3.7 Unit-test each feature on synthetic signals with known answers; for example, a 0.25
Hz sine wave must give 15 breaths per minute.
3.8 Save one feature table per night (Parquet format).
4.1 Build a baseline first: gradient-boosted trees on the epoch features, predicting
Wake, NREM or REM. This guarantees a working result early.

Phase 5 · RBD screening logic (Dec 14–27)
4.2 Build the main model: a 1-D convolutional network reads each epoch's 10 Hz

epochs across the night. Output: Wake, NREM or REM.
4.3 Use a class-weighted loss, because REM is the minority stage.

4.8 Report Cohen's kappa against the sleep-lab stages, recall per stage and a confusion

Phase 6 · Dementia and autism modules (Dec 14–Jan 3)
Dementia module:
5.1 Collect candidate events: movement bursts (all nights) and vocal events (real
nights).
5.2 REM gate: keep events in epochs whose REM probability is at least τ . Choose τ  on
inner folds only.
5.3 Breathing-pause check: drop events that start within 10 s after a breathing
disturbance ends, and log them for the breathing summary.
5.4 Range gate: keep events from the sleeper's bins and leg zone; drop partner and pet
bins.
5.5 Nightly RBD index = surviving events per hour of REM-like sleep. Add night
features: event duration and vigor, overlap with vocal events, share of events in the
second half of the night, and the rate and periodicity of NREM movements (which
separates periodic leg movements).
5.6 Define a valid night: at least 4 hours of detected sleep, with the sleeper's bin locked
for at least 80% of it.
5.7 Subject-level inputs: the median and spread of the nightly index across valid nights,
plus intake answers (age, antidepressant use, the RBD single-question screen). CAP
provides only age, so the other answers enter in the clinical pilot.
5.8 Train two classifiers: L2-regularized logistic regression (baseline) and XGBoost
(main), both class-weighted. Calibrate with Platt scaling on inner folds.
5.9 Pick the threshold on inner folds to reach at least 90% sensitivity. In screening, a
missed case costs more than a referral that rules RBD out.
5.10 State CAP's limit plainly: it has one night per person, so Stage 1 tests the single-
night index. The 3 simulated versions of each night test robustness to setup, not real
night-to-night variation, which needs the clinical pilot.
6.1 Bed-exit detector: large movement, then the sleeper's bin empty for at least 5 s,
triggers an alert. Cancel it if breathing returns within those 5 s.
6.2 Send alerts to the caregiver's phone with Firebase Cloud Messaging, and measure
the end-to-end delay.

Hearing "higher risk of Parkinson's" is frightening, and there is no proven prevention
yet. Explain what RBD risk means in plain words, avoid alarming language, and in the
clinical pilot let a clinician deliver results.
Privacy and data security
Raw audio is never saved or uploaded. Only event labels, timestamps and nightly
features are kept.
Processing happens on the phone, local storage is encrypted, and any upload is opt-in.
Consumer health apps outside HIPAA fall under the FTC's Health Breach Notification
Rule, amended in 2024, which counts sharing health data without permission as a
breach.
If the free tier shows ads, use contextual ads only: never use health data for targeting,
and never include an ad tool that collects it. The FTC has penalized health apps for
sharing health data with advertisers.
Patient data from a clinical partner stays under that clinic's HIPAA rules and data
agreements.
Safety
Keep the chirp volume low. Use pediatric mode for children, who hear higher tones
than adults, and watch pets, which hear well above 22 kHz.
Present bed-exit alerts as a convenience, not a medical alarm, until testing proves
them reliable.
Keep the phone plugged in on the nightstand, never under a pillow, to avoid
overheating.
Regulation
An app that screens for disease risk is likely regulated by the FDA as medical-device
software, while basic sleep tracking faces lighter rules. Until cleared, present
NightEcho as a research prototype.
The likely path: an FDA Pre-Submission (Q-Sub) meeting backed by clinical-pilot data,
then a De Novo or 510(k) request.
